# The Tank: Transportation Revolution Investing Game | Interactive Website + Reading, Worksheet & Thesis

**Your students are the sharks.** It's 1792 to 1837, and every student has $1,000. Five promoters walk into The Tank to pitch real ventures of the Transportation Revolution. After each pitch, students grill the promoter and decide on the spot how much to invest. Four ventures really succeeded. One was a real failure, sold with a con artist's tricks. Can your class spot it before the money's gone?

It comes as a ready-to-run interactive website for your projector, plus a student reading with a check for understanding, an Investor Tracker worksheet and a thesis-writing activity. There's nothing to install and no student logins.

---

## How it works

1. **Meet the promoter.** Each one walks on stage with the year, the place, what they're selling, and a quick prospectus: what you'd be buying, how it makes money, and the promise.
2. **Hear the pitch.** A fully voiced 90-second pitch with word-by-word captions. As the promoter talks, illustrated **exhibits** appear on the easel (maps, diagrams, how-it-works art), so students see exactly what they're investing in.
3. **Grill the promoter.** The class picks 2 of 6 prewritten questions. The answers appear in the promoter's own voice, and they hide real clues: honest admissions, dodges, pressure tactics and vague promises.
4. **Make your offer.** Students invest anywhere from $0 to everything they have left, and record it on their Investor Tracker. It's final, and they won't hear the next pitch first. Money left over at the end is worth nothing.
5. **Where are they now?** A newspaper from the years that followed reveals each venture's real outcome. A **Why It Mattered** screen then covers the key concepts and the impact on the nation. The game ends with a **Big Picture** timeline that ties the whole Transportation Revolution together.

---

## The five pitches

- **The Turnpike (1792):** a dry, honest farmer pitches the Philadelphia–Lancaster stone toll road.
- **Slater's Mill (1792):** a whispering agent pitches the mill built from England's memorized secrets.
- **The Steamboat (1808):** a carnival barker pitches Fulton's boat and its New York monopoly.
- **The Erie Canal (1817):** a booming politician pitches a 363-mile "ditch" across New York.
- **The Northern Cross Railroad (1837):** a folksy Illinois booster pitches a railroad on the eve of the Panic of 1837.

The ventures, dates and facts are real. The promoters are fictional characters.

## Easy math

Every venture reveals how much it **made per $1** as a whole number, for example +$3, or −$1 for the bust. Students just multiply: **what I invested × made per $1**. No decimals and no percentages. Every student can do it.

## Skills students practice

- Judging persuasive claims against evidence
- Telling fact from hype, and spotting red flags like pressure tactics, vague promises and unnamed "satisfied customers"
- Cause and effect: how turnpikes, factories, steamboats, canals and railroads changed the economy and connected the nation
- Economic decision-making under uncertainty
- Reading comprehension and evidence-based argument writing

**Standards connections:** C3 Framework D2.His.1, D2.His.14, D2.Eco.1; CCSS RH.6-8.8 (distinguish fact, opinion and reasoned judgment), RH.6-8.2 / RH.9-10.2, and WHST.6-8.1 / WHST.9-10.1 (write arguments).

## Details

- **Grades:** 8–12 (U.S. History, the early republic and the market revolution)
- **Time:** 2–3 class periods. Day 1: the reading and Check Your Understanding. Day 2: the game and Where Are They Now. Day 3 (or homework): Build Your Thesis. The game alone takes about 60–75 minutes.
- **Tech:** a projector and a browser. No logins, accounts or downloads. For catch-up mode, one device per absent student.
- **Built to work together:** students can check the promoters' claims against the included reading, then use both the game and the reading as evidence in their thesis.
